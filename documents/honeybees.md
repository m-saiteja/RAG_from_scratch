# The Honeybee: Biology, Behavior, and Beekeeping

## Introduction

The Western honey bee (*Apis mellifera*) is one of the most studied insects on Earth. Native to Europe, Africa, and parts of the Middle East, it has been spread by humans to every continent except Antarctica. Honeybees are social insects that live in large colonies, build wax combs, and store honey. They are also among the most economically important pollinators in agriculture.

There are several other species in the genus *Apis*, including the Eastern honey bee (*Apis cerana*), the giant honey bee (*Apis dorsata*), and the dwarf honey bee (*Apis florea*). However, *Apis mellifera* is the species most commonly kept by beekeepers worldwide.

## Colony Structure

A honeybee colony is made up of three types of bees, each with a distinct role.

### The Queen

Each colony normally has a single queen. She is the only fertile female and her main job is laying eggs. During peak season she can lay 1,500 to 2,000 eggs per day. A queen can live three to five years, far longer than any other bee in the colony. She also produces pheromones that help hold the colony together and suppress the development of other queens.

### Workers

Workers are sterile females and make up the vast majority of the colony. A summer colony may contain 20,000 to 60,000 workers. During the active season a worker lives only about five to six weeks, while winter workers can live several months. Workers perform different jobs as they age, including cleaning cells, feeding larvae, building comb, guarding the entrance, and finally foraging for nectar, pollen, water, and propolis.

### Drones

Drones are male bees that develop from unfertilized eggs. Their only function is to mate with virgin queens. Drones do not have stingers, do not collect food, and do not take part in hive maintenance. In autumn, when resources become scarce, workers often evict the drones from the hive.

## Life Cycle and Development

Honeybees go through complete metamorphosis: egg, larva, pupa, and adult. The time needed to develop differs by caste.

| Caste | Days from egg to adult |
|--------|------------------------|
| Queen | about 16 days |
| Worker | about 21 days |
| Drone | about 24 days |

An egg hatches into a larva after about three days. Nurse bees feed the larvae, and the diet decides their future: a larva fed royal jelly throughout its development becomes a queen, while others receive a diet of royal jelly for only the first few days, then pollen and honey, and become workers. Once a larva is large enough, workers cap its cell with wax and it pupates.

## Communication: The Waggle Dance

Foraging bees communicate the location of food sources through a behavior called the waggle dance. It was decoded by the Austrian scientist Karl von Frisch, who shared the 1973 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for this and related work.

In the waggle dance, a forager returns to the hive and moves in a figure-eight pattern on the vertical surface of the comb. The straight middle section of the figure eight, called the waggle run, carries the information:

- **Direction:** The angle of the waggle run relative to vertical matches the angle between the food source and the sun's direction outside the hive.
- **Distance:** The duration of the waggle run is proportional to the distance to the food. Longer waggle runs mean farther sources.
- **Quality:** The more vigorously and repeatedly a bee dances, the better the food source tends to be.

Bees also use scent, touch, and sound to communicate inside the dark hive.

## Honey Production

Honey begins as flower nectar, which is mostly water, often 70 to 80 percent. Foraging bees carry nectar in a special crop, or honey stomach, and pass it to house bees back at the hive. The house bees add enzymes that break down complex sugars and then spread the nectar into comb cells. They fan their wings to evaporate water until the moisture content falls to about 17 to 18 percent, at which point the cell is capped with wax.

Producing honey takes enormous effort. It is commonly estimated that bees visit around two million flowers to make a single pound of honey, and a single worker produces only about one-twelfth of a teaspoon in her entire life. Colonies store honey as food for winter, when no nectar is available.

## Building Comb

Honeybees build comb from beeswax, which young workers secrete from glands on the underside of their abdomen. The wax is chewed and shaped into hexagonal cells. The hexagon is an efficient shape because it uses the least wax to enclose the most space while leaving no gaps between cells. Cells serve several purposes: raising brood, storing honey, and storing pollen, often called bee bread once packed and preserved.

## Pollination and Agriculture

Honeybees are generalist foragers and pollinate a wide range of crops. A commonly cited figure is that roughly one third of the food humans eat depends on animal pollination, with bees playing a major role. Crops such as almonds, apples, blueberries, cherries, and cucumbers benefit strongly from bee pollination.

In the United States, almond orchards in California require so many bees that a large share of the country's managed colonies is transported there each February. Commercial migratory beekeeping, where hives are moved by truck from crop to crop, is now a major part of modern agriculture.

## Seasonal Cycle

**Spring:** The queen increases egg laying as flowers bloom and the colony grows quickly. Strong colonies may prepare to swarm.

**Summer:** The colony reaches its peak population and honey is stored. Foragers work long hours during nectar flows.

**Autumn:** Egg laying slows, drones are evicted, and the colony prepares for winter by storing honey and propolis.

**Winter:** Bees form a tight winter cluster around the queen. They shiver their flight muscles to generate heat, rotating between the cooler outer edge and the warmer core, and they live on stored honey.

## Swarming

Swarming is how a honeybee colony reproduces. When a colony becomes crowded, workers raise new queens. Before the first new queen emerges, the old queen leaves the hive with roughly half of the workers and settles temporarily on a nearby branch or structure while scout bees search for a suitable new nest site. Scouts return and perform dances to advocate for different locations, and the swarm eventually reaches a consensus. Meanwhile, the original hive continues with a new queen.

## Modern Beekeeping

Modern beekeeping was transformed in 1851 when the American clergyman Lorenzo Langstroth patented a hive with movable frames. His design relied on the concept of bee space, a gap of roughly 6 to 9 millimeters (about three-eighths of an inch) that bees leave open as a passage and do not fill with comb or propolis. By respecting this spacing, Langstroth's frames could be lifted out for inspection without destroying the comb.

The Langstroth hive remains the most widely used hive design today. Typical equipment includes:

- A bottom board and entrance
- Boxes called supers, which hold frames
- A queen excluder, sometimes used to keep the queen out of honey-storage boxes
- An inner cover and outer cover
- A smoker, which calms bees by masking alarm pheromones

## Threats to Honeybees

Honeybee populations face several overlapping pressures:

1. **Varroa mites:** *Varroa destructor* is a parasitic mite that feeds on bees and spreads viruses such as deformed wing virus. It is widely considered the most serious pest of managed honeybees.
2. **Pesticides:** Some insecticides, especially neonicotinoids, can harm bees' navigation, foraging, and immune function even at sublethal doses.
3. **Habitat loss and poor nutrition:** Fewer wildflowers and less diverse forage reduce the quality of bee diets.
4. **Disease and pathogens:** These include American foulbrood, a bacterial disease, and the gut parasite *Nosema*.
5. **Climate change:** Shifting seasons can cause mismatches between flowering times and colony growth.

In 2006 and 2007, beekeepers in North America reported unusually high colony losses, with many hives found nearly empty of adult bees. This phenomenon was named Colony Collapse Disorder. No single cause was identified, and researchers concluded that a combination of stressors, including mites, pathogens, pesticides, and poor nutrition, was likely responsible.

## Conclusion

Honeybees combine remarkable social organization, sophisticated communication, and real economic value. Protecting them involves good beekeeping practices, controlling parasites, reducing harmful pesticide exposure, and planting diverse flowering habitat for both managed and wild pollinators.
